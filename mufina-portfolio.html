02 / How I work

How I work

The same four moves on every project, whether it's a healthcare app or a platform teardown.

01

Find the real problem.

The brief usually isn't it. Gentli wasn't about reminders. It was about staying independent without feeling watched.

02

Talk to the hardest user first.

The 68-year-old. The student on a 2GB plan. The designer with a deadline. If it works for them, it works.

03

Show your reasoning.

I write down the trade-off, not just the final screen. A pretty file with no explanation is hard to build and easy to undo.

04

Test it, then say what changed.

Real numbers. And if it's a concept, I say it's a concept.

On this page

The short version

  • Seniors forget doses and lose track of whether they already took one.
  • Caregivers want reassurance, not a surveillance feed.
  • I designed three honest actions, a one-tap check-in, and a family circle the senior controls. In testing, everyone completed the medication task.

What it is

Gentli is a healthcare app for two people at once: the senior managing their own medication, and the family member quietly worrying about them. Most medication apps are built for the first person and ignore the second. Gentli treats both as users.

The problem

Many seniors struggle to remember their medication. Caregivers worry between check-ins. The obvious fix is more reminders, more alerts, more clinical detail. But every app I looked at was already doing that, and the people I spoke to had stopped using them.

The problem wasn't a lack of alerts. It was that the alerts made people feel managed.

“I forget if I already took my medicine.”
“I just want to know my mom is doing okay.”

What I heard

I interviewed seniors and family caregivers, then went through the conversations until the same four themes kept showing up.

Simplicity won.

Every extra option was a reason to close the app. The seniors didn't want a better medication app. They wanted one they could use without thinking.

Independence came first.

One woman told me she didn't want her son checking on her every day. She wanted to be fine without him needing to.

Caregivers wanted reassurance, not reports.

One daughter said she didn't need a chart. She needed to know her mum was okay today.

Accessibility was the product.

Large tap targets, readable type, and clear hierarchy weren't polish at the end. They were the difference between usable and unusable.

The trade-off

Caregivers wanted more information. Seniors wanted less. If I built a feed, I'd make the caregiver feel better and the senior feel watched. I had to give one person confidence without giving them surveillance. Most of the design decisions came out of that sentence.

Three decisions

01

Take, snooze, or skip.

A reminder that only lets you say “taken” is a reminder that makes you lie. Adding “skip” keeps the record honest, and it takes some of the shame out of a missed dose. People stop using apps that make them feel bad.

02

A check-in you can do with one tap.

Instead of a symptom form, users tap an emoji. No typing, no small fields, no medical language. It takes seconds, and it gives the caregiver the only signal they actually wanted: she's okay today.

03

A care circle with permissions.

Family members can be invited, and the senior decides what is shared. Sharing is optional and reversible. That turns the caregiver from a monitor into a guest.

The solution

Gentli combines medication reminders, a daily check-in, a care circle, appointment tracking, and simple progress summaries into one calm experience.

  • Reminders: take, snooze, or skip, with a clear history of what happened.
  • Wellness check-in: emoji-based, one tap, no typing.
  • Care circle: invite family, set permissions, control what is shared.
  • Appointments: upcoming visits and reminders in one place.
  • Progress: adherence, wellness trends, and consistency, shown simply.

Visual system

Warm, not clinical. Soft colour, plain language, friendly illustration. I built a reusable Figma system with buttons, inputs, cards, navigation, status indicators, and progress components, plus colour tokens, a type scale, and spacing rules.

I also keep a component count on my résumé. The point wasn't the number. It was making the next screen faster to build than the last one.

What testing showed

Medication task completion100%
Overall task success95%
Average task timeUnder 2 minutes
Ease of use4.2/5
Felt supported, not monitored4.4/5

The 100% is the medication task specifically, the thing the app exists to do. The 95% covers every task in the session, including the care circle and appointments. I'd rather show both than pick the prettier number.

The number I care about most is still the last one. A 4.4 on “felt supported, not monitored” means the privacy decision worked. If I had built the feed, that number would have been lower.

What I'd do differently

I tested one session per person. That tells me whether the app is learnable. It doesn't tell me whether someone keeps using it in week three, when the novelty is gone.

Next time: a two-week diary study, and a version where the senior and the caregiver disagree about permissions. That disagreement is the interesting part, and I only designed for the easy case.

What stayed with me

The most important screen in Gentli isn't the reminder. It's the permissions screen. That's where the product decides what kind of relationship it's creating.

A healthcare app that protects independence, not just adherence.

On this page

The short version

  • Students use five apps and still don't know what to study today.
  • Mock scores feel like a verdict, so they never learn why they lost marks.
  • I designed one daily plan of three explained tasks, a way to turn mistakes into revision cards, and a comeback plan for the days after a lapse.

An honest note first

This is a concept project. I wrote the research plan but didn't run the interviews, so the findings below are hypotheses, not evidence. I kept them labelled because I'd rather show the thinking honestly than pretend I have data I don't.

If you're reading this as a hiring manager: the tested projects on this site are Gentli and, informally, AI Assist. This one is a design argument.

The problem

Students don't fail because they lack content. They fail because they can't decide what to do next, and because their weaknesses are invisible.

  • They use five or more apps and channels, with no single source of truth.
  • Mock scores feel like a verdict, so they don't know why marks were lost.
  • Motivation collapses after two or three missed days, and most apps respond with guilt.
  • Many study on budget phones with patchy data, in 15 to 30 minute windows.
  • Many think in Tamil but are tested in English.

The goal: replace a pile of apps with one daily decision-maker. One next action, always explained.

The research plan

Twelve to fifteen aspirant interviews. A one-week diary study. A competitor audit of Unacademy, Testbook, Embibe, and BYJU'S. App-review mining.

None of that happened. What follows is what I expect to find, based on the competitor audit and the reviews I read.

Students juggle five or more appsOne home with a daily plan of three tasks
Mock scores feel like a verdictFeedback over scores, mistakes tagged by cause
Motivation collapses after missed daysA guilt-free comeback plan
Cheap phones, patchy data, short sessionsOffline-first, low-data mode
Thinking in Tamil, tested in EnglishTamil, English, and Hindi support

Priya

Priya is 24, works in a shop, and studies on the bus. She thinks in Tamil and writes her mock tests in English. She has four study apps and opens whichever one sent the last notification.

Her goal is to clear the cut-off with a routine she can actually keep. Her frustration is that she's busy, not lazy, and every app treats her like she has all day.

How it's organised

Four tabs you can reach with a thumb, plus an AI tutor that's available everywhere instead of hidden in its own section.

  • Home: daily plan, streak, exam countdown.
  • Practice: drills, mock tests, previous-year questions, revision cards.
  • Insights: topic accuracy, mistake causes, predicted score.
  • Me: exam setup, language, downloads, reminders.

The moment that matters

The third missed day.

Most apps break your streak and call it motivation. ScoredUp gives you a ten-minute plan and doesn't mention the streak. The first missed day shouldn't become the last one.

The comeback plan. The product is kindest at the moment the student expects to be punished.

Four decisions

01

Explain the why.

Every recommendation shows its reason. A student is more likely to follow a plan they understand, and more likely to forgive the product when it's wrong.

02

One question per screen.

Large tap targets and a single decision. In a 15-minute session on a cheap phone, every extra choice costs attention.

03

Accessible by default.

High contrast. Labels that are never hidden. Colour is never the only signal.

04

Healthy gamification.

Progress rings and milestones. No leaderboards that make struggling students feel worse than they already do.

The look

Blue for trust and primary actions. Orange for progress, streaks, and the active tab. A warm serif keeps the headings academic. Poppins keeps the body clear in both Tamil and English.

What success would look like

There's no usage data yet, because it's a concept. These are the numbers I'd test for:

Time to first practice question90 sec
Daily plan completion3 of 3
Day-7 retentionBeat norm
Mock-to-mock score changeUpward

What I'd do next

Test a clickable prototype with five aspirants. Refine the daily plan. Design the screens I left out: mock-test results, revision cards, the weekly report, and the comeback plan.

What I learned

Explain the why. A plan without a reason is just a notification.

Design for the worst phone and the shortest session. That constraint made every screen better.

Guilt is a design material. It's not an edge case. It's the reason students disappear.

A suggestion appears in context. It explains the rule, and it waits.

On this page

The short version

  • You've been in the file for three hours. The spacing is off by 4px in two places. One button fails contrast. The auto layout is broken in a way you've stopped seeing.
  • You find out on Thursday, in review, or worse, from a developer.
  • I designed an assistant that flags those things while you work, explains each one, and lets you decide. Guide, don't override.

The problem

Designers make small, avoidable mistakes under deadline. Inconsistent spacing. Misused auto layout. Contrast that fails accessibility. Nobody catches them in the moment, because they're too close to the work.

Then the review happens, or the developer asks a question, or QA files a ticket. By then the fix costs more than the catch would have.

  • Mistakes surface late.
  • Fixing them adds another round of iteration.
  • Inconsistent files create confusion at handoff.
  • Junior designers don't learn the rule, they just learn they got it wrong.

The cost is time, quality, and trust.

Who it's for

Early to mid-level UI/UX designers. People who work in Figma every day, ship under deadline, know the principles, and still miss the small stuff when they're tired.

“I don't know what's wrong until someone tells me.”

The design challenge

  • The mistakes are invisible while you're in flow.
  • Manual reviews are slow and depend on someone else's time.
  • Too many suggestions feel like nagging, so people turn them off.
  • Designers want help without losing control.

What it does and doesn't do

It does: watch layout patterns as you work, flag inconsistencies and accessibility risks, and explain each suggestion in context.

It doesn't: design screens for you, change a layer without permission, or enforce a rule you didn't agree to.

The principle: guide, don't override.

Before and after

Before: finish a screen, miss the issues, get feedback late, rework it, answer questions at handoff.

After: issues appear while you design. You fix them or dismiss them. The file is cleaner when it leaves your hands.

The feedback

I showed the prototype to six designers and asked for honest feedback. It wasn't a moderated test and there was no survey, so I don't have a percentage to quote, and I'm not going to invent one.

Six opinions are useful for spotting a bad idea. They're not proof that the idea works. What I'd want to test properly: whether someone can find a suggestion, understand it, dismiss it, and undo a change without losing their place.

That's the study I'd run before claiming any impact.

What I expect it to change

Fewer inconsistencies. Faster feedback loops. Better files. Fewer revision cycles. Junior designers learning the rule at the moment they break it.

Those are expected outcomes, not measured ones. The distinction matters on a portfolio.

What it doesn't solve

AI suggestions won't fit every context, and designers still need judgment. Unusual layouts may get misread as mistakes. There's a version of this that becomes another voice telling you your work is wrong, and that version would fail.

Next: team-level consistency checks, design-system alignment, and feedback that adapts to the designer's skill level. Also a way to say “I know, I meant that” that the assistant remembers.

What I learned

AI works best as a silent collaborator. Designers don't want automation. They want a second pair of eyes that doesn't take over the mouse.

Not all friction is bad UX.

This project has no screens. It started with a comment I kept seeing under creator videos.

On this page

The short version

  • Users want to download Pinterest videos for offline use and reuse.
  • Most videos are creator-owned, so unrestricted downloads create legal and trust risk.
  • The right move isn't a universal button. It's a creator-controlled download, with permission visible at the moment of saving.

What Pinterest is

A discovery platform. People save ideas for later instead of watching in the moment. Most of the video is creator-owned. Pinterest is the distribution layer, not the owner.

Users, reasonably, expect a download option anyway. That expectation runs straight into how the platform actually works.

What users want

  • Save videos for offline access.
  • Reuse video ideas easily.
  • Stop relying on shady third-party download tools.

What Pinterest has to protect

  • Most videos aren't owned by Pinterest.
  • Unrestricted downloads raise the risk of copyright infringement, unauthorised reuse, and losing creator trust.
  • The business depends on sending people back to creators, not redistributing their work.

The conflict

Users wantQuick access
Pinterest has to protectCreator ownership
Users wantOffline saving
Pinterest has to protectLegal compliance
Users wantEasy reuse
Pinterest has to protectPlatform trust

Pinterest is choosing the long-term health of the creator ecosystem over short-term convenience. That's a product decision. It isn't a missing feature.

The insight

Not all friction is bad UX.

I know how that sounds. Friction is usually the thing we're hired to remove. But here, the missing button protects creators, discourages misuse, and keeps Pinterest a discovery platform instead of a video library.

Sometimes the most responsible thing a product can do is refuse.

The evidence

Under creator videos, users keep asking the creator directly:

“Can I use this video?” · “Is it okay if I download and use this?”

That comment is doing a lot of work. It tells you three things:

  • People want the download.
  • They know the video belongs to someone.
  • They're unsure enough to ask permission.

The intent is already there. The mechanism isn't.

What happens if you add the button

  • Content gets reused without credit.
  • Creators publish less original work.
  • Legal complaints go up.
  • The platform loses credibility with the people who make the thing it distributes.

The damage lands on creators and on Pinterest. A short-term win for the user turns into a long-term loss for everyone.

A version that could work

Instead of one universal download button, creators could enable downloads per video. The permission would be visible at the moment someone saves. The file would carry attribution. The creator decides, and the decision is attached to the thing being downloaded.

01 / Save

User saves a video.

→
02 / Permission

The creator has allowed downloads. The card says so.

→
03 / Credit

The download carries the creator's name and a link back.

That balances the user, the creator, and the platform. The user gets offline access where the creator allows it. The creator keeps control. Pinterest enables use without endorsing misuse.

What I took from it

Good UX isn't just about reducing effort. It's about making a decision that holds up against legal constraints, creator trust, and the long-term health of the platform.

Timely disclosure beats a warning after the fact. A quiet reminder beats an aggressive restriction. And the job doesn't end when the user finishes the task.

The main thing: I didn't design a feature here. I argued against one. That turned out to be the more useful skill.
